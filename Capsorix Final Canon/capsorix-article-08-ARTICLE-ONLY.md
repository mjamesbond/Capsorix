# Innovation Is Not an Engineering Discipline

*The passage from an uncertain possibility to an operable system does contain one. Its material is not the idea, the prototype, or the technology. It is the set of commitments an organisation cannot take back.*

## An Organisation That Has Everything Except the Capability

A ministry, a bank and a manufacturer can hold identical inventories. An innovation unit with its own budget line. A portfolio of ideas ranked by a scoring model. A quarterly event that produces prototypes. Four of those prototypes demonstrating well enough to be shown to a board. An architecture team with opinions. A consultancy report recommending a platform. Two years later, one of the three has a system that people use to do work they previously did some other way. The other two have longer inventories.

The usual explanations reach for talent, culture, leadership commitment or budget. In our experience those variables are frequently present on both sides of the outcome, which means they cannot be doing the explanatory work. Something narrower separates the organisation that produces a working capability from the one that produces evidence of effort. It is not creativity, and it is rarely competence in any individual discipline. It is what happened to a small number of decisions.

This article was commissioned inside our own publication to test a claim we have been drifting toward for seven articles: that innovation is an engineering discipline. The claim, stated that way, is false. It is false for a reason that turns out to be more useful than the claim would have been, and the reason points directly at the small number of decisions.

The seven articles preceding this one were each, without saying so, an argument about the same thing. That organisations run on undocumented human systems. That transformation is organisational redesign rather than software delivery. That ambitious ideas differ in kind and must be classified before they are built. That decisive product work happens before implementation. That a great deal must be understood before technical commitment becomes responsible. That most demand for artificial intelligence is a symptom of an unexamined system rather than a justified intervention. Read together, all seven are arguments about **when it becomes responsible to close an option**. That is the object this article names, tests, and then declines to promote into a discipline.

## Why the Word "Innovation" Refuses to Be Engineered

Innovation is not a property an artefact has when it leaves the workshop. It is a verdict, and the verdict is passed by people who did not build the thing.

Three features of the word resist engineering. It is retrospective: whether something was an innovation is a judgement made after adoption, sometimes years after. It is relational: the same artefact is an innovation in one institution and an irrelevance in the one next door, because innovation is a statement about a relationship between an artefact and a context, not about the artefact. And it is conferred: value, meaning and legitimacy are granted by users, regulators, competitors and time, none of whom accept design specifications.

"Engineering innovation" is therefore a category error of the same shape as engineering a reputation or engineering a verdict. You can engineer the thing that is judged. You cannot engineer the judgement.

This is not a philosophical nicety. It has an operational consequence that explains a great deal of what goes wrong. An organisational unit charged with "producing innovation" has been made accountable for an outcome it does not control. Accountability of that kind is unstable. It resolves, predictably and without anyone intending it, into accountability for the things the unit can control, which are activities: sessions run, ideas captured, prototypes built, demonstrations delivered. The drift toward measuring activity is not a failure of character. It is what happens when you assign a verdict as a target.

So the working title fails. What survives the failure is the question the title was reaching for. If the verdict cannot be engineered, what about the thing the verdict is passed on? Something happens between an uncertain possibility and a system running inside a real institution. That interval is where the difference between our three organisations lives, and there is no reason in principle why it should be exempt from engineering.

## Engineering Was Never a Claim About Certainty

The most common objection to engineering language in this territory is that innovation involves creativity, politics, adoption, timing and emergent effects, none of which can be predicted, and that engineering implies a level of control nobody has.

The objection assumes something about engineering that is not true and has never been true.

A structural engineer cannot predict the loads a building will carry, the variation in the concrete that arrives on site, or the uses the building will be put to in fifty years. None of that is knowable. The engineer responds not with prediction but with margin, redundancy, codes derived from accumulated failure, staged inspection, and explicitly designed failure modes so that when the structure is overwhelmed it fails in a way that can be survived. Uncertainty is not eliminated. It is bounded, priced, and made someone's responsibility.

Billy Vaughn Koen, writing on the epistemology of engineering practice, characterises the engineering method as the use of heuristics to produce the best change in a poorly understood situation within the resources available.[^1] Walter Vincenti, examining the actual history of aeronautical practice, showed that much engineering knowledge is not handed down from science but generated by design activity itself, as engineers build things they do not yet fully understand and learn from the results.[^2] Neither account contains a promise of predictability. Both describe a practice defined by acting before you know, and remaining answerable for what follows.

That is the useful definition. **Engineering is accountable action under acknowledged ignorance.** Its characteristic instruments are margin, reversibility, staged exposure, testing, and a documented chain of who assumed what. Certainty was never on the list.

Which means uncertainty is not a reason to withhold the word from innovation work. If the word is wrong here, it is wrong for some other reason. The remaining reason is the one that matters: an engineering discipline requires an object. Something must be worked on. Civil engineering works on structures under load. Software engineering works on executable systems and their properties. What, exactly, would this work be working on?

## Uncertainty Is Not a Material Until It Meets a Commitment

Several candidates present themselves, and most fail on inspection.

*The socio-technical system* is too large. Nobody engineers a system they do not control, and the people who use a public service, the incentives acting on them and the authority structure they sit inside are not available to be designed. *The transition* describes an interval rather than an object. *Translation* names a location where things go wrong, not a material. *The evidence system* is an instrument, not the thing worked on. *The intervention* is closer, and is genuinely designed, but designing interventions is what service design, policy design and product management already do.

*Uncertainty* is the most attractive candidate and the most misleading. Uncertainty on its own is not a material, because it is not costly. Uncertainty in a domain where nobody is going to act costs nothing at all. It becomes expensive at exactly one moment: when someone acts in a way they cannot undo.

That moment is the object.

Define a **commitment** as an act that removes options from the future at a cost to reverse. Commitments in this kind of work fall into recognisable classes. Contractual: a signed licence, a three-year support agreement, a supplier chosen. Architectural: a data model, an identity scheme, an integration pattern, a published interface. Organisational: a team hired, a department restructured, a role created and filled. Regulatory and legal: a policy interpretation encoded into a rule that the system now enforces. Public: an announcement, a promise to citizens, a date given to a minister. Human: staff trained, a workflow changed, a habit formed, trust spent.

Each of these costs something to reverse, and the costs differ by orders of magnitude. A feature flag can be flipped in an hour. A data model that has been in production for two years with migrated historical records cannot be flipped at all; it can only be lived with or rebuilt. A public promise to a constituency cannot be reversed even in principle, only apologised for.

This is the claim the rest of the article rests on. **Innovation work spends two currencies: money and irreversibility. Only one of them appears in the budget.** Every organisation tracks what it has spent. Almost none can produce a list of what it has already made impossible to undo, or say what it knew at the time it did so.

The economics of this is not new and we do not claim it. Since the 1970s it has been understood that under uncertainty, irreversibility carries a real cost, and that deferring an irreversible commitment has positive value whenever waiting produces information that would change the decision.[^3] Software architecture has its own vernacular version, the instruction to defer decisions until the last responsible moment. What is missing is not the idea. What is missing is a practice that tracks commitments across the boundaries where digital systems are actually built, in organisations where the person taking an irreversible step is very often not the person who understands what it forecloses.

One case fits this framing least comfortably and deserves explicit handling. Sometimes the capability comes first: a technique becomes possible, a cost curve moves, and only afterwards does anyone ask what it is for. Nothing here requires an externally observed problem as a starting point. What changes is what the early commitments are made *to*. A team that commits to a retrieval architecture because a model became cheap enough to run over its own archive has foreclosed something real, and the evidence that justified it was evidence about feasibility rather than demand. The demand question does not disappear. It arrives later, and it must arrive before the expensive commitments, or the work becomes a technology holding out for a permission slip. We hold this part of the argument more loosely than the rest.

## The Two Ways Organisations Get the Ratio Wrong

If the object is the commitment, then discipline has a definition. **An initiative is disciplined when the irreversibility of each commitment is proportionate to the strength of the evidence that justifies it.** Failure is disproportion, and it comes in two forms.

The first is **premature closure**. An organisation signs a multi-year platform agreement before anyone has modelled the workflow the platform is meant to serve. It selects an architecture in the first month, when almost nothing is known, because a decision was requested. It announces a launch date publicly before the integration has been attempted. In each case the irreversibility is not knowledge-shaped; it is procurement-shaped, calendar-shaped, or political-shaped. The evidence arrives later, and by the time it arrives it can no longer change anything. The reliable symptom is research conducted after the architecture has been chosen, which is not research. It is documentation.

The second is **structural theatre**. Steve Blank named the phenomenon of visible innovation activity that produces no innovation, and the name has stuck because everyone recognises it.[^4] We want to sharpen it, because the usual framing treats it as insincerity and therefore suggests a remedy that never works.

Theatre is better defined structurally: **innovation activity that has been organisationally prevented from producing irreversible commitments.** Consider what a chartered innovation unit typically cannot do. It cannot sign a contract with an operational consequence. It cannot hire into a line function. It cannot amend a regulation or a standing rule. It usually holds a project budget rather than a recurring operating budget, which means it cannot own anything after it exists. A unit constrained in that way can only produce outputs that remain reversible, because reversible outputs are the only kind it has the authority to produce. Prototypes are reversible. Workshops are reversible. Pilots that end are reversible. And a commitment that is never made cannot become an innovation, because innovation is what happens when something is not taken back.

Seen this way, the people in the innovation unit are behaving correctly. They are producing the only class of output their mandate permits. This is why replacing them changes nothing, and why the units are so often staffed by capable and increasingly demoralised people.

Now the observation that we think matters most, and that we rarely see stated. **These two failures usually occur inside the same organisation at the same time.** The organisation is endlessly reversible where learning would be cheap, at the level of ideas, prototypes and pilots, and prematurely irreversible where reversal is ruinously expensive, at the level of procurement, platform selection, data models, organisational design and public promises. The two failures sit in different departments, run on different governance calendars, and are never compared, which is why the organisation experiences them as unrelated problems: one about innovation, the other about delivery. They are the same problem, and the diagnosis only appears when commitments across the whole initiative are laid out together.

## Commitments Are Made Inside Translations, Not Inside Meetings

If commitments are the object, the practical question is where they come from. The intuitive answer is that they come from decision forums: steering committees, investment boards, architecture review. That answer is wrong often enough to be dangerous.

Most commitments are made by competent practitioners doing ordinary work, at the moment they translate something from one form into another, and are not recognised by anyone as decisions.

A field is made mandatory in a schema because the form on screen required it, and eighteen months later the system cannot represent the routine case where the information does not exist yet. A stakeholder request is accepted as a requirement without anyone asking what condition produced the request, so the system now solves a stated problem rather than a real one. An identity model assumes one person equals one account, colliding permanently with a workflow in which a clerk acts on behalf of a department and needs to be seen to have done so. A sort order, a name-handling rule or an interface language is chosen for reasonable technical reasons and quietly determines who can use the system at all. A permission model encodes the current authority structure and, by encoding it, freezes it, so that a later reorganisation becomes a migration project.

Call these **silent commitments**: irreversible choices made inside a translation, by someone acting competently within their own discipline, that are not recognised as decisions by anyone, including the person making them.

There is good theory for why this happens at exactly these points. Paul Carlile's work on knowledge boundaries distinguishes boundaries where a shared vocabulary suffices, boundaries where meaning must be actively translated, and boundaries where interests genuinely differ and knowledge is at stake, which require transformation rather than transmission.[^5] Transformation is costly and uncomfortable. It requires someone to give something up. When the cost is high and the deadline is close, the boundary tends to get crossed by assumption instead, and an assumption crossed silently is a commitment made silently.

The practical consequence sets the direction for the whole practice. The instrument of disciplined innovation work is not a stage gate at the end of a phase. Stage gates arrive after the commitments have been made and ask whether the result looks acceptable. The instrument is a way of making irreversibility visible at the boundary where it is created. In concrete terms: architecture review that asks what a choice forecloses, not only whether it scales; requirement intake that records the observed condition rather than the request; a written record, kept while it is still cheap, of what would have to be true for this decision to be wrong. None of that is exotic, and almost none of it is standard.

## Operability Includes the Ability to Stop

A system that has launched is not finished, and treating launch as the terminal condition is how organisations produce artefacts that are technically live and institutionally dead.

An **operable system** satisfies six conditions. It has a named owner with the authority and the recurring budget to change it. It has a maintenance path, meaning a specific person or team who can safely alter it after the builders have gone. It has a support route for the exceptions the system does not handle, because there are always exceptions and they will otherwise be handled by the people least equipped to do so. It has a governance route for decisions the system cannot make on its own. It has an evidence loop that observes what actually happens after introduction, including effects nobody intended. And it has a defined way to be stopped.

The distinction between a system that works and a system that becomes embedded in daily practice is not a matter of degree. Implementation research has treated the two as separate problems for two decades, and the work of embedding a practice into routine use is not a residue of good design but a distinct kind of labour with its own determinants.[^6] We are borrowing a finding rather than a method, and the transfer from healthcare implementation to institutional digital systems is an analogy rather than a proof.

The sixth condition is the one nobody writes down, and it is where the ethical questions stop being a separate topic and become architecture. Who bears the cost when the system is wrong, who can contest a decision it makes, who maintains it when its builders have moved on, and who has the authority to switch it off are not questions to be answered in a governance appendix. They are answered, whether deliberately or by default, in the permission model, the retention policy, the exception workflow, the rollout order and the ownership of the credentials. Access control encodes authority. Retention encodes institutional memory. Rollout order decides which group of people absorbs the defects. An appeals route either exists in the workflow or it does not exist at all. If those things are decided after deployment, they were not decided; they were inherited from whatever the implementation happened to imply.

A modest illustration of the standard, drawn as a composite from our own delivery practice: a handover document that names the owner, the support window, the custody of credentials, and the single feature that has been built but not yet accepted. Declared incompleteness is an engineering artefact. Discovered incompleteness is a defect. The difference between them is entirely a matter of whether someone wrote it down while it was still cheap to say.

## A Programme That Could Not Commit

*The following case is a composite, assembled from patterns observed across several institutional engagements. It is not a description of any single organisation.*

A large public institution establishes a digital unit with a mandate to accelerate service modernisation. It is well staffed. Its budget is capital rather than operating, which is normal for a unit created by a transformation programme and is the first fact that decides its fate. It cannot hire into the line functions whose services it is meant to modernise. It cannot amend a procedural rule. It can commission, build, demonstrate and advise.

In its second year it produces a genuinely good piece of work: a redesigned application flow for a licence, based on real observation, that reduces a multi-visit process to a single submission. It is tested with actual applicants. It works.

It does not go into service. The obstruction is a rule about who may certify a submitted document, held by a directorate that reports elsewhere and has no stake in the unit's targets. Changing that rule is the only commitment that matters, and it is the one commitment the unit was structurally prevented from making. So the unit does the rational thing. It moves to the next prototype, because prototypes are the outputs it is authorised to produce.

Three years later the inventory is impressive and the service is unchanged. An external review concludes that the unit lacks delivery focus, which is precisely wrong. The unit had focus. It lacked the authority to make anything permanent, and everything it produced was therefore reversible by construction.

Two observations resist the easy reading. The first is that the work was not wasted. The observation the unit did on the licence process became the only accurate model of that workflow anyone in the institution possessed, and it materially reduced the risk of a procurement that happened later. Value was created; it was simply not the value that was being measured. The second is that no individual behaved badly. Every participant acted competently within a structure that had determined the outcome at the moment the mandate was written and the budget was classified as capital.

Diagnosing this as a culture problem, a talent problem or a motivation problem leads to interventions that cannot work. The diagnosis that leads somewhere is narrower and less comfortable: this unit was designed so that it could never spend irreversibility, and innovation is what happens when something is not taken back.

## A Team That Spent Irreversibility Deliberately

*Also a composite, drawn from institutional platform work in contexts where authority, trust and language are all contested. It includes what went wrong, because a case without failure is a brochure.*

A team is asked to build a platform through which citizens can raise matters with elected representatives. The initial request arrives as a feature list: profiles, a submission form, a tracking number, a dashboard, a map.

The team declines to select an architecture in the first month. This is unpopular and is the first deliberate act of the project. Instead they spend six weeks establishing what actually happens now, which turns out to involve intermediaries, informal escalation through personal relationships, and a widespread and reasonable expectation that a written complaint will disappear. The evidence changes the intervention. The problem is not the absence of a submission channel. It is the absence of any credible signal that a submission was received by a person with the standing to act on it.

They then sequence their commitments by cost of reversal, cheapest and most informative first. They reproduce the process by hand for two weeks with a small group, using no software, which is nearly free to undo and produces evidence nothing else could have produced. Their first genuinely irreversible commitment is a data model for a narrow slice, taken because a question about identity, naming and constituency boundaries could not be resolved by further discussion and had to be settled in a structure. They defer the multilingual interface commitment, judging that it will be cheaper to pay for rework in the presentation layer than to encode a language assumption into the data layer. That judgement cost them real rework later and they consider it correct.

The expensive irreversible commitments come last, and in a deliberate order: migrating real records, training staff, publishing a public address, and making a public statement about response times. None of those happens until the operability conditions have names attached, including who holds the credentials and who is funded to answer the exceptions.

They still get things wrong. The notification model assumes a device per person, which does not hold for a significant part of the intended population. That commitment was cheap to reverse, which is the only reason the error was survivable, and it was cheap to reverse because it had been recognised as a commitment at the time it was made rather than discovered as an assumption afterwards.

One question remains unresolved and cannot be resolved by engineering. The governance route depends on a specific person's authority. Whether the arrangement survives a change of leadership is not knowable, and pretending that a well-designed system is indifferent to who runs the institution would be the kind of false confidence this article is arguing against.

## What This Does Not Replace

An integrative claim is dangerous in a specific way: it tends to expand until it implies that the integrator does everything and the specialists execute. That mythology should be refused explicitly.

**Software engineering.** Nothing here makes a technical decision better. Software engineering owns correctness, performance, security, maintainability and the properties of running systems, and those are deep specialisms that no amount of breadth substitutes for. What changes is when the specialism is present. If most silent commitments are made in translation, and if the translation into architecture is where the least reversible ones are made, then engineering judgement is required at the beginning of the work and not at the point where a design is handed over for implementation. Treating implementation as downstream labour is not merely disrespectful; it guarantees that the decisions with the longest shadow are made by people who were not in the room when the question was framed.

**Product management.** The overlap is substantial and pretending otherwise would be dishonest. Good product leaders already sequence decisions by reversibility and already refuse premature architecture. Where emphasis differs: product management characteristically optimises what to build next for value, while the account given here optimises the order in which options are closed. These usually agree, and where they disagree it is in institutional contexts with heavy procurement, regulation and human-process dependencies, where the expensive commitments are not product commitments at all. Much of what is proposed here is a formalisation of what strong product leaders call judgement. Formalising judgement is worth doing only if it makes the judgement teachable, and that remains unproven.

**Design.** Design is not a preliminary visual stage and does not become one here. It frames the problem, produces the interaction through which the intervention exists at all, and is often the only discipline that reliably examines the person on the other side of the system. Design also makes silent commitments, and heavy ones: a flow that assumes a literacy level, a form that assumes a stable address, a metaphor that assumes a familiarity with an interface convention.

**Systems engineering.** The closest established relative, and stronger than this article in every respect where requirements can be specified, decomposed and verified. Its weakness in this territory is that it presumes a requirement exists to be engineered. Where the requirement is itself unknown, contested between parties with different interests, and likely to change once the first version exists, its methods have less to grip.

**Consulting.** Advice ends at a recommendation. The responsibility discussed here begins where the recommendation meets a schema, a rota, a budget classification and a regulation. But implementation alone does not constitute a discipline. Building what was recommended is delivery.

The difference worth preserving is between **specialist authority**, which is depth in a field and the right to be deferred to within it, and **integrative responsibility**, which is accountability for coherence across fields. The integrator has no authority over any specialism and should be prevented from acquiring it. Their authority is narrower and, properly held, quite sharp: the right to ask what a choice forecloses, and the obligation to record the answer where others can see it.

The obvious objection is that responsibility without authority is a well-known way to produce someone who is blamed for outcomes they could not affect. It is a fair objection and we do not have a clean answer. The best we can say is that the responsibility described here is narrow enough to be discharged in writing rather than by decree, and that where an organisation will not act on a recorded foreclosure, the problem being revealed is not the role design.

## The Term Is Already Taken

"Innovation Engineering" is not an available phrase. It exists as a registered mark and an established programme, developed by Doug Hall of Eureka! Ranch with the University of Maine from around 2005, taught there as an undergraduate minor and a graduate certificate, and built on a Deming-influenced system of skills for creating, communicating and commercialising ideas.[^7] It is a serious body of work with a substantial following. Its emphasis, on the throughput of ideas toward commercialisation, is close to the opposite of the emphasis argued for here. The phrase is also used loosely across engineering-management and design-engineering education to mean several different things.

An institution that adopts the phrase as its category label therefore inherits someone else's definition, invites confusion with a curriculum it does not teach, and raises a question about a registered mark that a publication is not the right place to answer.

The honest conclusion is a small one, and we are stating it against our own interest, having used the phrase about ourselves. **Name the object, not the field.** It is defensible to say that the sequencing of consequential commitments from uncertainty to operation is engineering work, that it has an object, and that it can be done well or badly. It is not defensible to announce membership of a discipline that has not been constituted, particularly under a name that already belongs to someone else's discipline.

## What Would Have to Be True for This to Be a Discipline

A discipline is not a process with a name. It requires an object of practice, units of analysis, methods, standards of evidence, recognised failure modes, professional responsibilities, boundaries, and a body of knowledge that accumulates rather than recirculating.

This work has the earlier items. A candidate object that is observable and ownable. Units of analysis: the individual commitment, the boundary at which it is made, the intervention as the aggregate. Failure modes specific enough to be recognised in the field: premature closure, structural theatre, silent commitment, and operability deferred past the point where it can still be designed. Boundaries, stated above.

It lacks two things, and they are the two that matter. It has **no standard of evidence**. There is no accepted answer to the question of how much evidence justifies a given magnitude of irreversibility, and without such a standard, proportionality remains a matter of taste dressed in careful language. And it has **no professional accountability**. A structural engineer can be publicly wrong and bear a professional cost for it, and that fact is what makes the field a discipline rather than a craft with a literature. Nothing comparable exists here. Nobody has ever lost a licence for making an institution dependent on a system that could not be maintained.

There is a further risk that should be named rather than managed. Formalising this work could easily make organisations worse. A commitment review is one small step away from becoming a stage gate, and stage gates reliably favour proposals that are legible over proposals that are important, punish the admission of uncertainty, and convert a thinking practice into a compliance ritual. Any organisation that turns the ideas here into a scored template will have reproduced precisely the failure the article set out to diagnose.

The verdict, then, is neither that innovation engineering is a discipline nor that the label is empty. It is **an integrative practice with a defensible object and no evidentiary tradition**. Whether it becomes more than that depends on whether practitioners begin recording what they foreclosed and what they knew at the time, in a form that accumulates across projects and organisations. That is a question about the next decade of practice, and an article that settled it by assertion would be doing the thing it criticises.

## Three Questions That Reveal Whether the Work Is Real

The argument reduces to three questions, which are useful in inverse proportion to how comfortable they are to ask.

**What have we already made irreversible, and what did we know when we made it?** Most teams cannot answer the first half of this question at all. Producing the list is itself the diagnostic: contracts signed, data structures in production, interfaces published, staff trained, promises made, roles created. Setting each item beside the evidence that existed at the time it was fixed reveals the shape of the initiative faster than any status report.

**What is the next irreversible thing we are about to do, and what justifies it?** This is not the same question as what is next on the roadmap. A roadmap sequences work. This sequences foreclosure. If the next irreversible commitment is large and the evidence supporting it is analogical, from a case study, a vendor demonstration or a competitor's apparent success, the correct move is usually to find a smaller commitment that produces better evidence, not to proceed more carefully.

**Who will own this in operation, and how would we stop it?** If neither has a name, the work is not yet aimed at an operable system, whatever its technical maturity. This question also distinguishes the two failure modes cleanly. A team that cannot answer it because no owner exists anywhere in the organisation is doing theatre, and the fault lies in the mandate rather than the team. A team that cannot answer it because the question has not come up is doing product development that will shortly become someone else's operational problem.

None of these three questions measures innovation. They cannot, for the reasons the article began with. What they establish is whether the work is structurally capable of producing an innovation, which is a different question and the only one that can be answered in advance.

## The Obligation the Word Carries

There is a reason to keep the word engineering here even after conceding that innovation cannot be engineered, and it is not the reason usually given. The word is worth keeping not because it promises control but because it names an obligation.

Structural engineers are not respected because buildings never fail. They are respected because when one does, there is a chain of decisions, a record of what was assumed, a margin someone chose, and a person who is answerable. The discipline exists in the traceability, not in the absence of failure.

The work between an uncertain possibility and an operable system has no such chain. When an ambitious institutional system quietly fails to become part of anyone's working life, there is usually no record of what was foreclosed, when, by whom, or on what basis. Ideas are attributed to the people who had them. Launches are attributed to the people who shipped them. The decisions that actually determined the outcome, made in schema design, in a procurement classification, in a mandate written a year earlier, are attributed to no one and written down nowhere.

Nothing prevents that record from being kept. It requires no framework, no certification and no new department. It requires treating irreversibility as something worth tracking as carefully as money, and treating the moment of foreclosure as the moment that deserves the attention currently given to the moment of launch.

Whether any of this eventually deserves to be called a discipline will be decided by whether practitioners start keeping that record and comparing it across projects. Until then it is a practice with an object, a set of failure modes, and an unfinished obligation. The obligation is the part worth taking seriously, because it is available immediately and does not depend on the argument being right.
