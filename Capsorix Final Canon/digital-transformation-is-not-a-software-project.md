In the previous article, we described a pattern we have found in nearly every organization we have worked with: somewhere inside the company, one person is quietly serving as the software. The routing logic, the exception handling, the institutional memory, the judgment about what to do when the standard case does not apply — all of it lives in a single head rather than in a system.

That observation carries an implication most companies move past too quickly.

If a person can be the software, then software was never what made the company work.

Something else was. And until that something is understood, no system will transform anything.

## What Organizations Actually Run On

Ask an executive what the company runs on and the answer usually names systems: the ERP, the CRM, the finance stack, the ticketing platform. This is a reasonable answer. Those things are visible, expensive, and procured through a formal process, which makes them feel foundational.

They are instrumentation. They are not the machine.

What a company actually runs on is a smaller and far less visible set of things:

- How decisions are made, and by whom.
- What causes work to begin, and what signals that it is finished.
- How work passes between people, and where it waits.
- Who is permitted to make an exception, and on what authority.
- What information travels, to whom, and how quickly.
- What behaviour the incentives genuinely reward, regardless of stated policy.

This is the operating model. It exists in every organization, whether or not anyone has written it down — and in most organizations, no one has. That absence is precisely how a person ends up becoming the software.

An operating model is not a strategy. Strategy describes what a company intends to achieve. The operating model describes what a company is actually capable of doing, repeatably, on an ordinary week. The gap between the two explains most strategic failure, and no software has ever closed it.

## Software Amplifies. It Does Not Substitute.

The most durable observation about software inside organizations was made by Melvin Conway in 1968: systems tend to mirror the communication structures of the organizations that produce them. Conway's Law is usually invoked by engineers discussing architecture. The broader reading is more useful to executives — software is a cast taken of the organization that commissioned it. It sets, and it holds the shape it was poured into.

Two mechanisms explain why.

**Encoding hardens.** To put a rule into software, someone must state it explicitly. A convention that previously lived in conversation, adjustable at the speed of a negotiation, becomes a constraint enforced at the speed of a machine. When the rule is sound, this is exactly the value software provides. When the rule is a historical accident nobody has re-examined, the accident has just been made permanent and given the authority of a system. Nothing defends a poorly designed process more effectively than the sentence *the system will not allow it*.

**Throughput multiplies.** Automation raises the rate at which a process produces whatever it produces. If the process reliably generates rework, disputes, and reconciliation, the company now receives those outputs faster and in greater volume. Bill Gates made this point plainly decades ago: automation magnifies the efficiency of an efficient operation and magnifies the inefficiency of an inefficient one. The observation is old, uncontroversial, and routinely ignored at the moment it matters most.

Together these explain a result we encounter often: a transformation programme delivers a technically sound system, on a defensible timeline, and produces no measurable change in how the business performs. Nothing was wrong with the software. The software was asked to do the one thing software cannot do — supply an operating model the company never designed.

## The Idea Is Old. The Failure Is Structural.

None of this is a new discovery. In 1990, Michael Hammer argued in *Harvard Business Review* that companies were using computers to accelerate existing processes rather than to redesign them, and that this squandered the technology's actual potential. More than three decades later the argument is unchanged, well documented, widely taught — and still largely unlearned.

When an idea this well-established fails to take hold this consistently, the cause is not ignorance. It is structural. Four forces work against it.

**Software can be purchased. Organizational design cannot.** A platform can be procured against a budget line, on a schedule, under contract. No procurement process can buy a decision about who holds authority over pricing exceptions. One is a transaction. The other is a negotiation with your own organization, and it has no vendor.

**Software projects have completion criteria.** A system launches. There is a date, a demonstration, a status that turns green, a slide for the board. Redesigning how decisions are made has no launch event and produces no artifact that can be presented as finished. Organizations reliably prefer work that can be shown to be complete.

**Redesign touches authority.** Every workflow encodes who decides. Changing a workflow therefore changes who decides — which makes it a political act before it is a technical one. In most companies, technical difficulty is discussed openly in meetings. Authority is not.

**The advisory market is compensated to build.** Studios, integrators, and platform vendors — ourselves included — are paid for systems delivered. Very few are paid to tell a client that the problem is not a missing platform but an unexamined operating model, and that the correct next step is smaller, cheaper, and considerably harder. We think this is worth stating plainly in a publication produced by a company that sells software.

## Two Ways to Get It Wrong

The principle that follows from all of this is that technology should faithfully reflect operational reality rather than attempt to replace operational thinking. *Faithfully* is carrying significant weight in that sentence, and it is worth separating the two symmetrical ways companies fail it.

**The first failure is encoding fantasy.** The system is built to match the documented process — the version on the flowchart, reviewed, approved, and inaccurate. Real work has always contained exceptions, side channels, informal escalations, and judgment calls that the diagram omits precisely because they are difficult to draw. Software built to the diagram meets reality within weeks. The result is familiar: shadow spreadsheets, side agreements, parallel record-keeping, and eventually quiet abandonment. The system is not used. It is worked around. And the person doing most of the working around is, as we described previously, the one who was already functioning as the software.

**The second failure is encoding accident.** The organization avoids the first mistake by studying how work genuinely happens and reproducing it exactly. This feels rigorous, and it produces a system everyone recognizes on first use. It also installs permanently whatever the company happened to drift into — an approval step added after an incident a decade ago, a handoff that exists only because two departments once reported to different executives, a reconciliation that compensates for a data problem long since fixed. Fidelity has been achieved. Nothing has been transformed.

Fidelity to reality is necessary. It is not sufficient. Between the two failures sits the actual discipline: understand the operating model as it genuinely runs, decide deliberately what it should become, and only then encode the result.

Most transformation programmes skip the middle step. It is the only step that constitutes transformation.

## The Order of Operations

The sequence matters more than any individual activity within it.

**Observe the operating model as it actually runs.** Not interviews about policy, which return the documented version. Observation of work. Where does work wait, and for whom? Who is telephoned when the standard case does not apply? Which decisions require a meeting to be scheduled? Which records are maintained twice because someone does not trust the first copy?

**Decide what the operating model should be.** Explicitly, in writing, with named owners, before any system is specified. This is a leadership act rather than a technical one, and it is the part that cannot be delegated to a vendor — including to us.

**Encode the decision.** Now software has something faithful to reflect: an operating model that was chosen rather than inherited, and that the organization has already agreed to operate.

Two diagnostic questions are worth applying before any transformation budget is approved.

*What decision will be made differently after this ships?* If no one in the room can name one, this is a software project. That may be entirely legitimate — replacing an unsupported system or retiring genuine technical risk is real work. It is simply not transformation, and it should not be funded, staffed, or measured as though it were.

*If the software were removed tomorrow, would the business still know how to operate?* If the answer is yes, the software is leverage: it makes a well-understood operating model faster, cheaper, and more reliable. If the answer is no, the software is not leverage but a hiding place — the company has quietly outsourced the design of its own operations to a vendor's default configuration.

## Transformation Is an Act of Invention

There is an uncomfortable implication in all of this.

If digital transformation is the deliberate redesign of how an organization operates, then it cannot be documented into existence. Documentation records what already is. Redesign proposes what is not yet — a way of operating that has never been tested inside this particular company, with these particular people, under these particular constraints.

That is not an administrative exercise. It is an act of invention, and it carries the risks invention always carries. It can be wrong. It can be rejected by the organization it was designed for. It can be entirely right and still fail on execution.

Which raises the question this publication takes up next. If the redesign of an organization is fundamentally an act of invention, and software is the mechanism by which that invention becomes durable and repeatable, then the decisive capability is not implementation. It is the ability to take something that exists only as an idea — an operating model, a product, a way of working that no one has yet seen function — and make it real without losing whatever made it worth building.
